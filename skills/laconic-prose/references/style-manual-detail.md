# Australian Government Style Manual: editing rules

Distilled from the seventh edition at https://www.stylemanual.gov.au/ (Commonwealth of Australia, CC BY 4.0), scraped 13 September 2026. The full text of all 186 pages sits in [australian-government-style-manual.md](australian-government-style-manual.md); grep that file for a rule this page does not cover. Do not read the full corpus end to end, it is about 1.3 MB. Each page's own Evidence and References appear there under `### Sources for this page`, so the manual's authority for a given rule is checkable.

For citing Australian legislation, cases, ATO rulings and explanatory memoranda, read [aglc4-citation.md](aglc4-citation.md) instead. AGLC governs inside a citation; this page governs the prose around it.

## When to apply it

**Apply in full** when the draft is Australian Government, public sector or Australian corporate content, when the user asks for Style Manual compliance, or when they ask for plain language.

**Apply the mechanics only** (spelling, capitals, numbers, dates, punctuation) to any other Australian English prose. These settle questions the core skill leaves open.

**Do not apply the register rules** to a personal blog, fiction, a chat reply or any draft whose voice is the point. The Style Manual is a standard for government content, not a licence to flatten someone's writing. The core skill's order still governs: the user's explicit requirements, factual fidelity, reader comprehension, the writer's voice, then stylistic preference.

The Style Manual is evidence, not a trump card. Cite it when a plain language edit needs backing. Never cite it to justify an edit the reader does not need.

## The Style Manual's own list of writing tics

The manual's editors publish the habits they catch in their own drafts. The list is close to a plain language restatement of this skill's pattern catalogue, and it is quotable authority when a writer pushes back:

- staccato sentences
- overused idioms and metaphors
- assumed technical knowledge
- writing things in 3s
- hedging phrases such as "I think" and "would you be able to" instead of being direct

It also warns against editing to satisfy a tool: "Don't edit and revise your writing simply to improve a readability score. Use the tool's editing suggestions to identify potential issues, then exercise judgement." Treat word lists and scanners the same way.

## Plain language and word choice

- Choose everyday words. Unfamiliar words slow readers and exclude people with low literacy, disability or English as a second language.
- Target Australian year 7 reading level (WCAG 3.1.5, reading level AAA). About 44% of Australian adults read at level 1 or 2; only 1.2% read at diploma level. Readers with high literacy also prefer plain language because they are time-poor.
- Remove jargon, slang and idioms. "In light of new information" becomes "in response to new information".
- Use "we", "you" and "us" when the register allows. "We will assess your application within 30 days" beats "Applications are assessed within 30 days".
- Use a verb rather than the noun built from it: "apply", not "make an application"; "decide", not "reach a decision".
- Avoid double negatives. "It was acceptable", not "it was not unacceptable".
- Explain technical terms or supply a glossary. Do not remove a term the audience actually uses.

## Sentences

- Average 15 words. Maximum 25. Break a longer sentence in two, or move part of it into a list.
- Standard order is subject, verb, object. Wrong word order misinforms: "I have a meeting that John arranged in the seminar room" has two readings. Split it or add a comma pair.
- Use active voice. Passive is legitimate when the receiver matters or the actor is unknown, but name the actor in an "agentless passive" such as "the reform was implemented" whenever you can.
- Write positive sentences. "Include these documents when you apply", not "you can't submit your application if you don't include these documents". Negative imperatives are fine when research supports them ("Don't accept friend requests from strangers").
- Cut words that carry no function. Strip a sentence back to subject, verb and object, then rebuild it, leaving out anything that does not earn its place. Adverbs and adjectives are the usual candidates.
- Drop empty "there is" and "there are" constructions.
- Avoid noun trains: no more than 3 adjectives or nouns in a row. "The human resource reallocation analysis" becomes "the analysis of how we can reallocate staff".
- Vary sentence length deliberately. Short sentences limit the scope of an idea; readers still want rhythm and variety. This is not a licence for stacked fragments.
- Specific traps: don't use "if" and "unless" in one sentence; make "other than" scope obvious; replace "such steps as are appropriate" with "appropriate steps"; use "and" rather than "being".
- Starting a sentence with "And", "But" or "Or" is allowed to keep sentences short. Don't overdo it.

## Paragraphs and structure

- One topic per paragraph. Start a new paragraph rather than introducing a second topic at the end of one.
- Open each paragraph with a topic sentence or a transition sentence. In a topic sentence, make the paragraph's subject the grammatical subject.
- Most paragraphs run 2 or 3 sentences. Media releases use 1 or 2; long-form reports allow up to 6. A single-sentence paragraph is effective in moderation and loses force when overused.
- Order paragraphs by a logical sequence: steps, importance, cause then effect, problem then solution, pros then cons.
- Default to the inverted pyramid: main point first, then detail in descending order of importance. Readers decide within seconds whether to keep going, and often scan only a fifth of a page.
- Pick the structure to match the content. Hierarchical for grouped topics, sequential for steps and events, narrative for a story told start to finish.
- Combine structures when the document needs it: organise a long report by topic, then put each section's finding before its detail. Use chronology when the sequence explains the events or the task; preserve it in a personal narrative.
- If you copy every first paragraph into one place, you should get a usable summary. If a main point is missing, rewrite a first paragraph.

## Headings

- 70 characters maximum, including spaces.
- Sentence case. Capitalise the first word and proper nouns only. No full stop. No all caps. No underline.
- Front-load keywords. The first 2 or 3 words may be all anyone reads.
- State the main point. "Learn how to drive", not "More information".
- Avoid questions as headings; they push the main idea to the end and add cognitive load. A genuine FAQ entry is the exception.
- Keep the hierarchy complete: one H1 per page, never skip a level, stay at 3 or 4 levels, give each level at least one sibling, and separate every heading from the next with at least a sentence of text.
- Make headings at the same level grammatically parallel: all noun phrases, or all instructions.
- Number headings only for a series of steps.
- Avoid shortened forms in headings unless better known than the full term, such as DNA or CSIRO.

## Lists

- Use a list for a series, never for one item. Keep lists short and don't stack too many; content with too many lists is hard to follow.
- Parallel structure is mandatory: same word type to start each item, same tense, same sentence type. Move any word repeated across items up into the lead-in.
- Minimal punctuation. No semicolons or commas at the end of items. No "and" or "or" after items, except where it is critical to meaning, such as legal content.
- Never write "etc." at the end of a list. A lead-in of "for example", "such as" or "including" already says the list is incomplete.

Three list types, each with its own punctuation:

| Type | Lead-in | First letter | Full stops |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sentence list | Sentence with colon or full stop, phrase with colon, or a heading with no punctuation | Capital | After every item |
| Fragment list | Sentence or phrase, always ending in a colon | Lower case, unless a proper noun | Last item only |
| Stand-alone list | Heading only, no lead-in, no colon | Capital | None, not even the last |

A phrase lead-in plus any one fragment must form a grammatical sentence of 25 words or fewer. Phrase lead-ins always end in a colon; pick one punctuation mark for sentence lead-ins and use it throughout the document.

Numbered lists are for order that matters. Avoid in-line numbered lists ("the facilitator will 1) welcome participants and 2) introduce the speakers"): break them out, or link the sentences with "next". Avoid ordinals such as "firstly" and "secondly" for ordering general prose. Avoid multilevel lists; if forced, use 2 levels at most, lower case letters for a numbered second level and an en dash for a bulleted second level.

## Links

- Link only when it meets a user need. Links reduce readability and increase cognitive load.
- Write link text that describes the destination and makes sense out of context. Never "click here" or "more information".
- Front-load keywords and link only the keywords.
- Put links at the end of sentences so the reader finishes the sentence first.
- Linking to a file: give the document title (not the file name), the file type and the size, for example "*Australia in brief* [PDF 13 MB]". Prefer linking to the landing page over the file.
- Deep-link to the page the user needs, not the homepage. Link to a source rather than duplicating it.
- Use the email address itself as link text, not the person's name.
- Open links in the same tab by default. No exit modals.

## Voice and tone

Three levels of formality. Standard suits most content.

| Level | Contractions | Personal pronouns | Idiom, metaphor, humour | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Formal | No | Third person mostly | No | Legal writing, policy, reports, ministerial letters |
| Standard | Yes | Yes | No | Emails, letters, services, corporate communications, media releases |
| Informal | Yes | Yes | Yes, with care | Social media, blogs, some newsletters |

Formality does not change the plain language requirement. Slang is out at every level in government content.

Basic government voice is respectful, clear and direct, objective and impartial. In practice:

- Respectful: inclusive language, everyday words, speaks to "you", no sarcasm or name-calling, not condescending and not overfamiliar.
- Clear and direct: plain language, active voice, concise, structured. "We will not be funding any new projects this year", not "In-flight projects will be funded subject to FAS FMDC approval".
- Objective and impartial: relies on facts, excludes opinion, stays balanced. Watch adjectives, adverbs and viewpoint, which quietly convert fact into opinion. "There was 15 mm of rain last summer. This made it the driest summer for 120 years" is factual; "Only 15 mm of rain meant last summer was the driest we've ever had" is not.

Second person ("you") is active, direct and engaging. First person singular ("I") is rarely suitable outside correspondence.

Persuasive writing needs three parts together: facts and logic with cited evidence, emotional appeal grounded in real impact on people, and credibility shown through the writing rather than asserted.

## Australian English mechanics

### Spelling

- The Style Manual lets an organisation choose the *Australian concise Oxford dictionary* (ACOD) or the *Macquarie dictionary*. Laconic uses the *Macquarie dictionary* and its first spelling. Style Manual itself follows ACOD sixth edition.
- Use the first spelling in the entry, the headword. Record any deliberate departure in a style sheet.
- Style Manual departs from ACOD on a few forms: acknowledgement, lodgement and judgement keep the "e", but legal material uses "judgment".
- Use the dictionary for hyphenation, capitalisation, plurals, one word or two (webpage, not web page), and whether a term is flagged derogatory.

Choose a form by meaning and grammatical role, then keep it consistent. A spelling preference does not make every accepted alternative an error. Keep an explicit user choice and exact spelling in quotations, names, code and other protected literals.

| Use | Form and distinction |
| --- | --- |
| A permit or permission | licence is the noun; license is the verb; licensed is the inflected form. |
| An activity or professional business | practice is the noun; practise is the verb, with practised and practising. |
| A person supported by another | dependant is the noun; dependent is the adjective for something that relies on another. |
| Directing attention | Prefer focused and focusing; treat focussed and focussing as accepted variants that differ from the house preference. |
| Someone who gives advice | Prefer adviser in ordinary prose; preserve Advisor in an official title or name. |
| A person's welfare | Write wellbeing as one word. |

Check the sentence before changing a grammatical pair. A global replacement cannot determine whether practice names a business or describes an action, or whether a word belongs to a protected title. Check uncertain variants individually; do not invent a source's recommendation when it is unavailable.

Commonly confused pairs worth checking in any Australian draft: accept/except, advice/advise, affect/effect, complement/compliment, council/counsel, councillor/counsellor, dependant (noun)/dependent (adjective), discreet/discrete, disinterested/uninterested, elicit/illicit, foreword/forward, licence (noun)/license (verb), loose/lose, passed/past, personal/personnel, practice (noun)/practise (verb), precede/proceed, principal/principle, stationary/stationery, their/there/they're, weather/whether. Fixed forms often mangled: could have (not "could of"), should have, would have, deep-seated, free rein, to all intents and purposes, moot point, on tenterhooks, leeway, pique interest, regardless (not "irregardless").

Use "that" for essential information and "which", with a comma pair, for non-essential information.

### Capitals

- Minimise capitals. Sentence case for sentences and headings: first word plus proper nouns only.
- Generic references to organisations, titles and programs take lower case. "Australian Government" is capitalised; "the government" is not. "Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet", then "the department". "Advancing Renewables Program", then "the program".
- Capitalise an official title when it precedes the office holder's name: "Chief Defence Scientist Tanya Monro". Avoid capitals for a role in running text: "the senior policy adviser offered their view".
- Expanded forms of shortened terms follow ordinary capitalisation: NSW is New South Wales, but EIS is environmental impact statement.

### Percentages, quotes and titles

Three rules that come up constantly in Australian tax and accounting prose:

- **Per cent** is two words. "Percent" is not Australian spelling. "Per cent" is the adverb and "percentage" is the noun, so write "the percentage of Australians with degrees", not "the per cent of Australians". With the symbol, use a numeral and close it up: `12%`, `0.4%`.
- **Single quotation marks** are the default. Use double marks only for a quotation inside a quotation. Single marks also carry a word being used in a special sense, where italics would otherwise be tempting.
- **Act and case titles** are italic in running text and roman in a reference list, because a block of italics is hard to read. A bill is roman until it is enacted.

### Punctuation

- Use minimal punctuation: only what makes the sentence grammatical and the meaning clear. Heavy punctuation usually means the sentence is too long.
- No full stops after headings, page headers, footers or captions; between letters of an acronym; or after most abbreviations and contractions.
- One space after a sentence. Never two.
- Quotation marks: the punctuation goes inside when it belongs to the quote, outside when it belongs to the sentence. Replace a quote's full stop with a comma before an attribution: "'No, that's chicory my dear,' Fiona replied." When two marks compete, use the stronger one only.

### Dashes

This section supports the house rule against em dashes.

- **Spaced en dashes are Australian Government style for digital content.** Em dashes are grammatically valid and used by other style guides, but never mix the two for the same purpose in one document.
- If you do use em dashes under another house style, leave them unspaced. A spaced em dash opens too large a gap and interrupts reading flow.
- Use spaced en dashes sparingly, to set off non-essential information. Removing the content between a pair must leave a complete sentence.
- For spans, prefer words: "from 57 to 65 years", "between 1970 and 1980". Never mix "from" or "between" with a dash. Screen readers usually do not announce dashes.
- Unspaced en dash exceptions for date spans: financial years, calendar years, publication and program titles, terms of office and lifespans (both with 4 digits on each side), and anywhere space is limited. Technical content with many spans may use en dashes throughout.
- **House override, and it wins:** a financial year takes a hyphen, `2026-27`, not the manual's `2026–27`. Fix each one by hand; scripted rewriting of these spans is unreliable.
- En dash between coordinate nouns that keep their own meaning: Murray–Darling Basin, student–teacher ratio. A hyphen instead makes a compound noun with a different meaning: student-teacher.
- Codes: en dash U+2013 `&ndash;`, minus U+2212 `&minus;`, hyphen U+2010 `&hyphen;`, em dash U+2014 `&mdash;`. Do not substitute an en dash for a minus symbol.
- Two em dashes in a row mark an interruption in quoted speech or a deliberate omission: "S—— signed the deed in 2017."

### Hyphens

The manual sets few firm rules and defers to the dictionary. Remove a hyphen when the phrase reads the same without it; keep one that stops a misreading.

- **Set phrases stay open.** An adjective plus noun or noun plus noun modifier takes no hyphen: open source project, plain language edit, public sector content, tax office ruling, equal opportunity employer. Add the hyphen only when a further modifier makes the phrase ambiguous: a retrospective tax-office ruling.
- **Close up established compounds:** email, online, website, codebase, dataset, timeframe, roadmap, coordinate. Settle any other compound from the dictionary or this manual, not by habit.
- **Hyphenated coinages usually stand in for a claim:** AI-powered, data-driven, best-in-class, world-class, next-generation, user-friendly, future-proof. Say what the thing does, or cut the modifier. Keep a compound that is the precise term or part of a proper name.
- **Position matters.** A compound adjective takes a hyphen before its noun and none after it: "the up-to-date accounts" but "the accounts are up to date"; "small-business owners" but "owners of small businesses". Two adjectives and noun plus adjective compounds are the exception and keep it in both positions. Never hyphenate a compound modified by very, particularly, least or most.
- Hyphenate the compounds that misread without it: two adjectives (bitter-sweet), a noun plus an adjective (accident-prone, text-only), a participle compound (government-owned, well-known) and any numeral compound (a 4-part series, a 39-year-old man, two-thirds).
- An adverb in an adverbial role takes none, and an adverb ending in "-ly" never takes one: a newly discovered species, a fully loaded truck, phrases that are often empty. The only exceptions are fully-fashioned and fully-fledged.
- Prefixes keep the hyphen for doubled vowels (re-enter, pre-eminent), where it separates a different word (re-cover, re-creation), and in the established non-, self-, sub- and anti- forms. Close the rest up: bypass, overreact, coordinate, overfamiliar.
- Preserve a hyphen that changes the action: re-sign means sign again; resign means leave a position. If the intended action is unclear, query it before editing.
- Fixed compound nouns keep their established form regardless of position: go-ahead and mother-in-law. The before-noun test applies to temporary modifiers, not every compound.
- Hyphenate two-word numbers from twenty-one to ninety-nine when written as words, including that part of a larger number: one hundred and forty-five. Preserve numerals where the format calls for them.
- Settle an established compound from a source, not by ear. Where dictionaries disagree, grep the scraped manual and follow its dominant spelling: plain language 98 uses to 6, lower case 94 to 4, stand-alone 33 to 1, high-level 3 to 1.
- Hanging hyphens are hard to follow. Repeat the word: "full-time and part-time positions", not "full- and part-time positions".
- **Protected literals keep every hyphen:** paths, identifiers, code, URLs, citation pinpoints such as `s 8-1`, quoted text and the word lists in the pattern catalogue.

### Numbers

General content, not technical or scientific:

- Write "zero" and "one" as words; use numerals for 2 and above. Typefaces confuse 0 and 1 with letters.
- Exceptions using words: starting a sentence, fractions where only a general idea is needed ("about two-thirds"), proper nouns and titles ("Seven Hills"), figures of speech ("my two bob's worth"), and numbers below 10 in journalistic content such as media releases.
- Exceptions using numerals for 0 and 1: units of measurement, times of day, comparisons ("1 in 5 people, and 1 in 7 young people"), series of related numbers, tables and charts, dates and times, steps and instructions, age and school years, scientific content.
- Commas from 1,000 up, grouped in threes. Never a space: screen readers announce "2 500" as two numbers.
- Large rounded numbers from a million combine numerals and words: 1.4 million, $50 billion. Both "1 million" and "one million" are acceptable; use the numeral in technical, number-dense, series or currency contexts.
- Percentages take the symbol with a numeral: 1%, 95%. Money takes numerals and symbols: 20c, $1, US$20,000; words only for approximations ("thousands of dollars").
- Ordinals: first to ninth in words, then 10th, 21st, 22nd. Centuries take numerals: 1st and 2nd centuries.
- Always compare like with like. Don't write "over twice as high" beside "1.1 times as high".

### Dates and time

- Day month year, no commas: 22 July 2024, Monday 22 July 2024. Non-breaking space between day and month.
- Numeric dates only where space is tight: d/m/yyyy, for example 1/7/2020. Two-digit years only for financial data or where the century and order are unmistakable.
- Times use a colon and lower case am or pm with a non-breaking space: 8:30 am, 1 pm. Trailing zeros are optional but must be consistent.
- Don't pair "am" with "morning" or "pm" with "afternoon".
- Use "noon", "midday" or "midnight" rather than 12 am or 12 pm.
- Decades take a plain s: 1980s, not 1980's.

### Shortened forms

- Spell out an acronym or initialism on first use, with the shortened form in parentheses. Reverse it for well-known forms: "DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid)".
- No definition needed for NT, ACT, am, pm, Qantas, BHP, radar, scuba and similar.
- Shorten only terms that are well known or used many times. A form used once or twice should stay spelt out.
- Often clearer than an acronym: spell out the agency in full, then use the generic noun. "The Bureau of Meteorology ... the bureau".
- Repeat the full term in long content where a reader may arrive mid-page. Add a glossary when specialist forms are numerous.
- No full stops inside or after acronyms, initialisms or contractions, except where one ends a sentence. Australian practice drops the full stop that US English adds to "Mr." and "Ltd."
- Avoid the plural or possessive form at first definition.
- Prefer English to Latin: "for example" not "e.g.", "that is" not "i.e.". Where "e.g." and "i.e." are unavoidable, keep both full stops (screen readers need them) and never follow either with a comma.
- Grammatical contractions (don't, we're) suit standard and informal tone. Avoid them in formal content such as high-level briefs and responses to inquiries.

## Inclusive language

Mention age, disability, cultural background or gender only when it is relevant. Talk to the person, not their difference. This is a legal obligation under the Racial, Sex, Age and Disability Discrimination Acts.

- **Age.** "Older people", "older Australians", "seniors", never "old people" or "elderly". "Young people" or "youth"; take care with "youths", which often implies males and carries connotations. Refer to students by level of study, not age. Hyphenate age before a noun: "a 39-year-old man". Use numerals unless the age starts the sentence.
- **Disability and neurodiversity.** Use person-first language by default: "person with disability" (not "a disability"), "person who is deaf or hard of hearing", "person who is blind or has low vision", "person with mental illness". A person *has* a condition, they are not the condition: "Rupert has schizophrenia", not "Rupert is a schizophrenic". Avoid "handicapped", "hearing impaired", "wheelchair-bound", and euphemisms such as "differently abled". Don't frame people as heroes, victims or inspirational because of disability. Identity-first language ("disabled person", the Deaf community) is correct where a person or community prefers it. Use "person without disability" rather than "able-bodied".
- **Gender and sexual diversity.** Singular "they" is the default when gender is unknown or a person is non-binary. Better still, use "you", a plural subject, or drop the pronoun. Never "he/she". Avoid gendered job titles: police officer, firefighter, supervisor, actor, host, flight attendant. "Ms" by default; "Mx" only where a person states that preference. Don't ask for a title or gender unless the form is designed to collect it.
- **Cultural and linguistic diversity.** "Multicultural communities"; avoid "ethnic Australians" or "ethnic groups", which imply migrant heritage is unusual. Avoid the acronym CALD outside specialist audiences. Ask for "given name" and "family name", never "Christian name", "first name" or "surname". Drop "migrant" and "new arrival" once people have settled. Join a dual identity used as an adjective with an en dash: "the Japanese–Australian community", but "Japanese Australians" as a noun phrase.
- **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples.** Authority sits with the relevant community; consult rather than assume. Be specific: name the nation, island or community before reaching for a broad term. Use plurals for collectives (peoples, nations, cultures, languages), present tense, and strengths-based language. Never use "Aborigines", "Islanders", "ATSI", blood-quantum terms such as "half-caste", possessives such as "our Aboriginal peoples", or "myth", "legend" and "folklore" for First Nations beliefs. Don't italicise words from First Nations languages; they are Australian languages. Describe a language no longer spoken as "sleeping", never "extinct". Capitalise Welcome to Country and Acknowledgement of Country, which are distinct things. Follow the community's own spellings and capitalisation, including Elders, Country and Traditional Custodians where they prefer it.

Language changes. Check that your understanding is current before relying on any list.

## Accessibility

WCAG 2.2 is the baseline for Australian Government digital content, expressed as four principles: content is perceivable, operable, understandable and robust. The criteria that bear on prose:

- 3.1.5 Reading level (AAA): lower secondary level, Australian year 7.
- 3.1.3 Unusual words (AAA) and 3.1.4 Abbreviations (AAA): avoid or explain unusual words, phrases and idioms; expand acronyms on first use.
- 2.4.2 Page titled (A), 2.4.6 Headings and labels (AA), 2.4.10 Section headings (AAA): clear page titles and descriptive section headings.
- 1.3.1 Info and relationships (A) and 1.3.2 Meaningful sequence (A): real heading and list markup, content in a meaningful order.
- 2.4.4 and 2.4.9 Link purpose (A and AAA): link text that works out of context.

Practical consequences for editing: avoid double negatives; don't rely on colour, shape, size or position alone to carry meaning; keep punctuation minimal because screen readers handle it inconsistently; use commas rather than spaces in long numbers; and write out spans as words rather than dashes.

## Editing levels

Useful vocabulary when a user asks for "an edit" and the scope is unclear:

- **Substantive (structural) edit.** Structure, style, meaning, flow, gaps, plain English review, consistent voice and tone. Does not rewrite at length or fact-check.
- **Copyedit.** Consistency, accuracy and completeness against a style sheet. Does not restructure, rewrite for plain English or fact-check.
- **Proofread.** Quality control against a master copy after the copyedit. Queries rather than changes. Not a second round of editing.

A style sheet records every decision made along the way: dictionary, spelling variants, capitalisation, and any departure from the manual. Recommend one when a user has recurring content.
